Denibbing Lacquer And Varnish Layers Correctly

Master denibbing and between coats sanding for lacquer and varnish. Learn grits, abrasives, workflows, and tips for glass-smooth, defect-free finishes.
November 24, 2026 · 11 min read · by Mark Jensen  · in Between-Coats Sanding

Between Coats Sanding for Flawless Lacquer and Varnish

The light catches your cabinet door just as you set it on edge, revealing a constellation of tiny bumps you swore weren’t there an hour ago. Yesterday’s first coat flowed out beautifully; today, under a harsh raking light, dust nibs stand proud like grit in wet concrete. You run your fingertips across the surface—nitrile gloves amplifying tactile feedback—and feel the truth: the film is smooth in patches but pocked elsewhere. This is the moment that separates “good enough” from professional-grade. It has nothing to do with your sprayer settings anymore. It’s about what you do next—how you denib, how you scuff, and how you execute disciplined between coats sanding without erasing edges or telegraphing scratches into the final gloss.

We’ve all tried to cut corners: a casual pass with a worn pad, a quick tack-off, then another coat to “bury the nibs.” The result is predictable—trapped artifacts, dull halos, witness lines on edges, and a topcoat that refuses to achieve that deep, liquid look. Lacquer and varnish are honest systems. They record everything you do—every abrasive choice, every stroke angle, your pressure, your dust extraction, even your patience. Denibbing isn’t a chore wedged between fun parts; it is the hinge-pin of the finish schedule.

What follows is a precise, field-tested approach to denibbing and scuffing lacquer and varnish layers so your final surface reads as one continuous, optically flat film. We’ll profile abrasives that cut clean without clogging, outline workflows that respect each chemistry’s recoat window, and show you how to use light, touch, and disciplined technique to remove defects while preserving film build. If you’re ready to produce surfaces that look poured-on rather than painted-on, this is where the work gets quietly excellent.

Quick Summary: Expert denibbing and disciplined between-coats sanding—using the right abrasives, pressure, and dust control—turn good lacquer and varnish jobs into flawless finishes.

Why Denibbing Determines the Final Sheen

The quality of your final sheen is decided before the last coat dries; it’s settled at the denibbing stage. Dust nibs, overspray granules, and raised grain are protrusions that break the optical smoothness of a film. Each one scatters light, creating micro-highlights that read as texture and dull patches in satin or haze in gloss. If you try to “bury” those with subsequent coats, you increase film thickness, but the peaks remain unless you mechanically level them. Denibbing removes peaks selectively while maintaining the bulk of your build.

Two mechanics matter most: cut depth and scratch pattern. Cut too shallow and the nibs persist; cut too deep and you’ll expose the substrate or create witness lines—visible rings where you sanded through one layer but not the next. The goal is to cut just enough to return the film to a truly uniform plane. For nitrocellulose lacquer and most waterborne systems, intercoat adhesion is strong, but they are also easier to sand through at edges. Varnishes and oil-modified polyurethanes are tougher, with a narrower window between “gummy” and “powdery” sanding. Always test a corner. If the abrasive loads immediately or the film balls up, it’s not ready.

Optically, a random-orbit scratch pattern at P320–P400 will be obliterated by the next coat in a satin system; on a high-gloss clear, those same scratches can ghost through unless you refine to P400–P600 or use non-woven ultra-fine pads with consistent scratch profiles. Use raking light to confirm that you’ve removed protrusions (peaks) without over-leveling valleys. Work edges last and with less pressure; you can’t put film back once it’s gone.

Abrasive selection for between coats sanding workflows

Abrasives are not interchangeable consumables; they are engineered tools with coatings and backings that directly control cut rate, consistency, and loading. For between-coats denibbing, stearated aluminum oxide (AO) papers and film-backed discs are the workhorses. The stearate layer resists clogging in soft or semi-cured films, maintaining a predictable scratch. Film backers hold grain uniformly and keep edges crisp—critical for even scuffing.

  • Grit ranges:
    • Lacquer (nitro or pre-cat): P320–P400 for denibbing; P400–P600 for high-gloss scuff, especially on clear coats.
    • Waterborne acrylic/poly: P320–P400 dry; refined to P600 if chasing a gloss rub-out later.
    • Oil-modified varnish/poly: P220–P320 after full dry-to-sand; only move to P320–P400 when films are fully powdering.

Silicon carbide (SiC) excels in wet applications and on very hard cured films due to its sharper, friable grain. For intercoat work (not post-cure rub-out), reserve SiC chiefly for situations where AO clogs and you can validate adhesion remains uncompromised by moisture. Non-woven pads (maroon ~P320–P360, gray ~P600–P800) are excellent for complex profiles and light scuffing without pronounced scratch direction; use them with a soft hand to avoid burnishing which can reduce mechanical tooth, notably with waterbornes.

Foam-backed sheets and interface pads transform how abrasives conform to slight undulations. A 3–5 mm foam interface on a random-orbit sander prevents hard disc edges from gouging while allowing nibs to be cut with less pressure. That said, too-soft a pad can “dome” over nibs, skipping the peak rather than cutting it; keep the tool moving, and verify nib removal visually. Avoid worn-out papers—dull grain burnishes, generating heat and smear rather than a crisp cut.

Dry vs wet methods and dust control

Between coats sanding is primarily a dry process for one reason: you’re creating mechanical tooth and deleting defects in films that still need to chemically coalesce with subsequent coats. Introducing water or solvents during intercoat prep risks contamination and adhesion loss. Reserve wet sanding (SiC, lubricant) for post-cure leveling and rub-out when the film has fully hardened per the coating’s technical data sheet.

Dry scuffing with stearated AO on a dust-extracting random-orbit sander—backed by a multi-hole interface pad and connected to a HEPA extractor—offers three advantages: consistent scratch, low heat, and cleanliness. Multi-hole discs align with most pad patterns and keep the cut face clear; HEPA extraction reduces airborne fines that guarantee fresh nibs in the next coat. On flats, run low OPM (e.g., 4–6 on typical ROS dials) with minimal pressure; let the abrasive do the work. On moldings, switch to hand blocks or foam-backed sheets, and let the profile dictate your motion.

When is wet appropriate intercoat? Rarely. Some shops lightly damp-scuff waterbornes with gray pads to minimize dust; if you do, use distilled water, avoid surfactants, and allow full dry plus an alcohol wipe per the manufacturer before recoating. For oil-based varnish/poly, do not introduce mineral spirits as a sanding lube between coats; it can plasticize the film and slow cure.

Cut indicators matter. If the powder is fine and non-gummy, you’re good to proceed. If the abrasive loads quickly, wait longer. Tack cloths are polarizing: standard sticky tacks can leave residue that causes craters or fisheyes. Use low-tack, waterborne-safe cloths, or better, blow off with filtered air and vacuum with a clean brush. According to a article, many finishers target P220–P320 on polyurethane to remove dust bumps and promote adhesion—useful guidance, but always confirm against your product’s recoat window.

Workflow for lacquer and varnish systems

Different chemistries reward different rhythms. Align your between-coats plan with flash, cure, and recoat windows to maintain intercoat adhesion and sanding efficiency.

  • Nitrocellulose lacquer (sprayed)

    1. Flash 20–30 minutes between light coats. After two to three passes, allow 1–2 hours.
    2. Denib with P320–P400 stearated AO, ROS with foam interface. Two to three light passes; stop when nibs disappear.
    3. Vacuum and blow off; wipe with a clean, dry microfiber. Avoid solvents at this stage.
    4. Continue building thin coats to target film. For high-gloss, refine scuff to P600 before the last two coats.

  • Waterborne acrylic/poly (sprayed or brushed)

    1. Respect minimum dry-to-sand (typically 45–120 minutes at 70°F/21°C, 40–60% RH).
    2. Denib P320–P400 dry. Hand-sand edges to prevent burn-through. If using pads, gray non-woven can substitute on profiles.
    3. HEPA vacuum and a light waterborne-safe tack. Avoid silicone anywhere in the environment (swirl marks, fisheyes risk).
    4. Recoat within the manufacturer’s window; if you miss it, a more aggressive scuff (P320) may be required to regain tooth.

  • Oil-modified polyurethane/varnish (brushed or sprayed)

    1. Let cure fully to a powder-sand state—often 8–24 hours. Cool temps extend this.
    2. Denib P220–P320. Use a flat, cork-backed block by hand on flats; power sanders can wave soft films.
    3. Do not wet-sand between coats. Avoid tack cloths that can leave residue; vacuum + microfiber only.
    4. Recoat as specified. Missed window? A deeper scuff and solvent wipe per TDS may be necessary.

Actionable tips:

  • Index your work with pencil: Light cross-hatch on the surface; sand until marks just vanish, ensuring uniform cut without over-sanding.
  • Protect edges: Blue tape 1–2 mm back from sharp edges to prevent burn-through during ROS passes.
  • Control your light: Use a movable raking light at 15–30°; chase the light across the panel and sand only what you can see.
  • Standardize pressure: One-hand ROS grip; if you see pad stall or swirl halos, you’re pressing too hard.
  • Refresh abrasives: Swap discs every 1–2 panels; a fresh P320 outperforms a glazed P240 and leaves a safer scratch.

Troubleshooting nibs, witness lines, and defects

Even with disciplined denibbing, defects happen. The cure is a blend of diagnosis and targeted removal.

  • Persistent nibs after scuffing: Likely doming from too-soft backing or too-light pressure. Switch to a firmer interface or hand block and make two controlled passes directly over the nib at 45°, then 90°.
  • Witness lines on edges: You’ve cut through a layer boundary. These halos are most visible in clear gloss. Solve by feather-leveling the surrounding area with P400–P600, then applying two light coats, allowing enough flash for flow. Prevent with taped edges and hand-sanding edges only.
  • Fisheyes/craters after recoating: Often contamination—silicone, oil, or tack cloth residue. Strip contaminated coat if severe, or spot-fill with compatible glaze if minor, then scuff and recoat after a solvent-compatible wipe per the TDS. Purge the environment of silicone sprays.
  • Swirl marks that telegraph: Random-orbit patterns may show through high-gloss clears if scuffed too coarsely or under-cured. Refine to P600, align your final scuff with a consistent pattern (or use gray pads), and ensure sufficient flash and film flow in subsequent coats.
  • Overspray dryness/rough feel: Inadequate overlap, too much air, or atomization too fine. Sand to level the grit with P320–P400, then adjust gun: slightly larger fluid tip or reduced air, closer pass distance, and warmer substrate to encourage melt-in.

Consider using nib files or ultra-hard nib knives only on fully cured films; for intercoat work they often cut too deep, too fast. For trapped debris (a hair, for example), spot-level with a small P400 square wrapped on a pencil eraser, then expand the blend only as needed. Always re-evaluate with raking light and clean the surface thoroughly before you lay the next coat. The fewer “hero” moves you need, the more likely your finish will read as a single, flawless film.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q: What grit should I use to denib lacquer versus polyurethane?
A: For nitro or pre-cat lacquer, start at P320–P400 for typical denibbing and refine to P400–P600 before final high-gloss coats. For oil-modified polyurethane or varnish, use P220–P320 once the film sands to powder; only move finer after the film hardens fully and cuts cleanly.

Q: How do I know the film is ready for between coats sanding?
A: Make a small test pass in an inconspicuous area. If the abrasive produces dry powder and doesn’t clog, sand. If it gums, wait. Check manufacturer dry-to-sand guidance and remember that cool, humid conditions lengthen that window.

Q: Should I wet-sand between coats to reduce dust?
A: Generally no. Intercoat sanding is best done dry to preserve mechanical tooth and avoid moisture or solvent contamination. Reserve wet sanding for post-cure leveling and rub-out when the film has fully hardened.

Q: How do I avoid sanding through edges and creating witness lines?
A: Tape edges 1–2 mm back, sand edges by hand with reduced pressure, and keep the ROS away from sharp transitions. Work flats first, remove tape, then lightly scuff edges with a used fine abrasive.

Q: Can non-woven pads replace sandpaper for scuffing?
A: On profiles and complex shapes, maroon (≈P320–P360) and gray (≈P600–P800) pads provide a uniform scuff without directional scratches. On flat, high-gloss builds, film-backed P400–P600 often leaves a more predictable scratch that disappears under the next coat.
