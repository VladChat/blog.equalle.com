Microfiber Versus Foam For Final Pass

Learn when microfiber or foam wins the final pass for resin polishing. Expert guidance on surface prep, abrasive choice, heat control, and flawless finishes.
August 10, 2026 · 9 min read · by Mark Jensen  · in Resin Polishing

Microfiber vs Foam for Final Pass in Resin Polishing

The shop lights are low and unforgiving. You’ve already leveled the pour, refined the sanding marks, and chased dust nibs until your fingertips memorized every ridge on the piece. Under the inspection lamp, the surface whispers perfection—until you rotate it just a few degrees and a faint veil of micro-haze blooms across the gloss. It’s the final mile, the moment that separates a “nice attempt” from a specimen-grade finish. On your bench sit two contenders for the last pass: a plush microfiber finishing pad and a svelte, open-cell foam pad designed to jewell a surface without adding back defects. If you’ve ever questioned which one to mount before you pull the trigger, you’re not alone.

For anyone doing resin polishing—river tables, jewelry domes, pen blanks, cast parts—the decision becomes a calculated trade-off between refinement speed and the risk of reintroducing haze. Microfiber cuts fast and can erase shallow DA traces left behind at P3000–P5000, but its fiber geometry also raises local shear that can gray-out soft epoxies. Foam rides the peaks more gently, stabilizes temperature, and tends to amplify clarity, yet it may lack the bite to clear residual sanding signatures. The best finish comes from sequencing, not guessing: understanding how each pad interacts with your abrasive system, resin hardness, machine motion, and heat.

That’s what this guide delivers—no fluff, just the mechanics, process controls, and repeatable tests to choose correctly every time you face the final pass.

Quick Summary: Use microfiber when you still need light correction; switch to soft foam with reduced pressure and slower arm speed to jewel and maximize clarity on the final pass.

Defining the Final Pass: Microfiber vs Foam

The final pass is a low-pressure, low-heat refining step intended to maximize clarity without adding mechanical haze. It is not a correction step. The tools you choose—microfiber or foam—must match that intent.

Microfiber finishing pads use thousands of synthetic filaments to increase contact points across the surface. Each filament can act like a micro-blade when charged with a fine abrasive, generating higher localized shear and thus more cut than foam of equivalent “finish” grade. That extra bite helps if your surface still holds faint DA pigtails or a diffuse scratch field from P2000–P3000. But the same mechanical advantage increases the chance of fine marring on softer resin systems, especially those below full post-cure or with lower glass transition temperatures (Tg). Microfiber also loads swarf quickly; a clogged field of fibers will scuff rather than polish.

Foam finishing pads distribute pressure through a continuous cell structure, evening out micro-loads and dampening edge aggression. Open-cell, ultra-soft foams with rounded edges “float” across the surface and help stabilize pad temperature. They typically produce less haze and better “jeweling”—the optical refinement that deepens clarity—provided the substrate is already free of meaningful scratch patterns. The trade-off is reduced mechanical cut. If you’re still seeing directional scratches under raking light, foam can glaze over them rather than remove them.

In practice, the final pass is often a two-step micro-sequence: a brief, controlled touch with fine microfiber when light defects remain, immediately followed by a foam finishing cycle to bring up gloss and optical depth. The exact split depends on your abrasive finish line, resin hardness, and machine dynamics.

Pad Selection for Resin Polishing Workflows

Resin systems vary widely. Epoxies formulated for casting or coatings can present very different hardness and heat sensitivity, even after a week of cure. Polyester and UV-cure resins behave differently under shear. Your pad choice on the final pass should reflect the resin’s mechanical response and your scratch refinement up to this point.

If you finish sand to P3000–P5000 (Trizact or equivalent), many epoxies are already “correction-light.” In that scenario, a foam finishing pad paired with an ultra-fine, non-diminishing polish will typically deliver the cleanest final pass. Use just enough product to lightly lubricate the pad; excess carrier can smear, hide defects temporarily, and then outgas into a faint film.

For resins that are slightly harder or where your sanding left a uniform, but visible, P3000 trace, a short, single set with a finishing microfiber pad can help resolve the last residual haze. Keep pressure minimal—just pad weight plus a touch for stability—and monitor panel temperature. Immediately transition to foam with the same fine polish to jewel the surface.

Soft or under-cured epoxies are particularly prone to microfiber-induced micro-marring. If you must use microfiber, limit dwell time, lower orbit speed, and blow out the pad every section pass. Many professionals reserve microfiber for earlier refining and let foam own the last word in clarity.

According to a article, a reduced-pressure, slower-arm-speed final pass can elevate clarity, while an optional ultra-soft foam “jeweling” step helps on softer finishes. The same principle applies to resin: once correction is complete, drop the mechanical energy and let foam finalize the optics.

Machine Dynamics, Pressure, and Heat Control

Final-pass success is as much about kinematics as it is about pad type. Dual-action (DA) machines with 8–15 mm throws are preferred for resin finishing because their random orbit reduces directional marking and mitigates holograms common with rotaries. That said, a rotary running at low RPM with a very soft foam pad can deliver exceptional jeweling on hard resins—if you can keep heat tightly controlled.

Pressure is the first lever. Aim for 0.5–1.5 kg of downforce (roughly 5–15 N) on the final pass—just enough to keep the pad engaged without stalling a DA. Excess pressure collapses foam cell structure, spikes local shear under microfiber, and elevates temperature. Measure arm speed too; 2–4 cm per second is typically slower than your correction pace, allowing abrasive particles to burnish instead of plow.

Speed settings should be moderate to low. On many DAs, that’s 2–3 out of 6 for the final pass. Higher OPM can raise pad face temperature quickly, softening the top microns of an epoxy and making it more susceptible to smearing. If the surface feels tacky or the pad leaves a faint “grabby” trail, your thermal budget is blown; stop, wipe, and cool the panel before continuing.

Cleanliness and lubrication are next. Blow your pad with compressed air between each section, or use a brush for foam. A clogged microfiber pad behaves like 2000-grit paper; a saturated foam pad hydroplanes and fails to engage the abrasive. Use minimal polish: a thin film ensures consistent contact and reduces sling that can load edges.

Finally, subdivide the panel into small, manageable sections—roughly 30×30 cm for flat pieces or smaller for complex geometry. Overworking a large area makes heat and product control impossible. The goal is consistent, low-energy contact that refines the last micro-texture without introducing new artifacts.

Practical Tips, Testing, and Defect Avoidance

A disciplined, test-driven approach prevents surprises when you wipe that last panel. Here’s a field-proven workflow and specific tactics to make microfiber and foam work for you on the final pass.

  • Establish a scratch baseline: Stop sanding at P3000–P5000 equivalent with uniform, trace-level marks. If you see directional lines at arm’s length, you’re not ready to polish. Clean thoroughly to remove abrasive residue before switching to pads.

  • Run an A/B tape test: Mask a straight line, then perform a controlled set with microfiber on one side and foam on the other, using the same ultra-fine polish. Wipe, inspect under raking and cross-polarized light, and pick the clearer result. Use a cheap gloss meter if available—target an improvement of ≥2–3 GU in the foam jeweling step.

  • Control product load: Prime the pad lightly the first time (microfiber: brush in a skim coat; foam: thin film), then use two pea-sized drops per 30×30 cm section. If residue flashes slow or feels gummy, you’ve over-applied.

  • Limit section passes: For final jeweling, two slow cross-hatch passes are plenty. With microfiber, cap dwell at 15–20 seconds per section; with foam, 20–30 seconds. More time rarely improves clarity and often raises temperature.

  • Clean and cool: Blow out microfiber every section; switch to a fresh foam pad if face temperature is warm to the touch. Keep at least two finishing foams in rotation to avoid heat soak.

Common defects and how to avoid them:

  • Micro-haze on soft resin: Skip microfiber on final; drop DA speed; switch to a plusher foam with rounded edge profile.
  • Smearing or “grab”: Reduce product, clean pad, and let the surface cool. Consider extending cure time or mild post-cure if resin spec allows.
  • Trapped pigtails revealed after wipe: Back up one stage—re-sand the area with P3000 on a firm interface pad, then repeat polish sequence.

When in doubt, shorten the microfiber step and bias your energy toward foam jeweling. The last pass is about optics, not aggression.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q: How do I know if I should use microfiber or foam on the final pass?
A: Inspect under raking and cross-polarized light after P3000–P5000 sanding. If you still see uniform, shallow haze or faint pigtails, do a very short microfiber set first, then finish with foam. If no meaningful marks remain, go straight to ultra-soft foam with minimal pressure.

Q: What machine speed and pressure are ideal for jeweling epoxy?
A: Use a DA at low to mid-low speed (about 2–3/6), with 0.5–1.5 kg downforce. Move slowly—2–4 cm/sec—and limit to two cross-hatch passes. The pad should glide without audible chatter or drag.

Q: My surface looks perfect under shop light but hazy in sunlight—why?
A: You likely have micro-marring from excess shear or a pad loaded with residue. Switch to a clean, softer foam pad, reduce product, and make a brief, cooler jeweling pass. Reassess outdoors and under a single point-source light.

Q: Does pad thickness or edge shape matter on the final pass?
A: Yes. Thicker, open-cell foams with rounded edges distribute pressure and reduce edge aggression, minimizing haze on soft resins. Thin, firm foams or short-nap microfibers transmit more energy and can reintroduce fine marks if overworked.

Q: Can I final-polish uncured or just-cured epoxy?
A: Avoid it. Under-cured epoxy is mechanically soft and heat-sensitive; it will smear or micro-mar easily. Follow the manufacturer’s full cure or post-cure guidance before your final pass, and keep temperatures low throughout.
