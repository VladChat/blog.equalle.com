# Vacuum Extraction for Concrete Polishing Indoors

It usually starts with a plan that sounds simple: brighten a tired basement or workshop, refresh the slab, maybe erase those paint freckles and faint oil shadows. You picture a clean, glassy floor reflecting light back into the room. Then you pull the grinder from its case, hear the motor swell, and within seconds the space turns murky—like fog rolling over a coastline. The dust smells mineral, metallic, and somehow warm. It hangs in the light, settles on your jacket, and drifts toward rooms you never intended to touch. If you’ve ever started a concrete polishing project indoors without real dust control, you know how quickly optimism gives way to urgency.

Now imagine the same start, but different choices. Instead of an improvised shop vac, you connect a purpose-built extractor with a tight-fitting shroud and a 2-inch antistatic hose. The grinder spins, but the plume that used to bloom around your shoulders now dives directly into the shroud. The air stays clear, your respirator feels less necessary, and the room sounds like focused work—not crisis. You finish a pass, glance up, and the walls aren’t frosted with dust. When you break for coffee, you don’t track footprints down the hall. That is the quiet relief of getting vacuum extraction right for indoor work, especially during concrete polishing where the dust load and runtime are both high.

This isn’t just about neatness. Airborne silica can harm lungs and linger in HVAC systems. It can settle into cracks and cabinets and take days to fully remove. Good dust extraction respects your time, your health, and the finish you’re trying to achieve. And it’s more than buying a “bigger vacuum”—it’s an ecosystem of airflow, filters, hoses, and work habits that move dust from tool to bag, safely and predictably. Done right, you’ll get the clean, polished floor you pictured—and a house or facility that doesn’t look like a jobsite exploded inside it.

## The hidden mess of indoor grinding

Grinding concrete indoors puts you in a closed loop with everything you make—especially dust. Concrete dust is a mixture of fine cement particles, aggregate fragments, and, critically, respirable crystalline silica small enough to ride indoor air currents and stay aloft. Unlike sawdust, these particles don’t just settle quietly; they drift, re-entrain as you walk, and can hitch a ride into adjacent rooms through tiny pressure differences. That’s why indoor grinding without a capture plan can leave a film on windows 30 feet away and a gray line under every door.

The mechanics matter. A typical 7-inch hand grinder can remove surprising volumes of concrete in minutes. Every pass frees micro-particles that want to spread. The moment that dust escapes the shroud, you’re depending on gravity and guesswork. But capture at the source flips the physics in your favor. When the shroud seals well against the slab and the vacuum provides enough airflow (CFM) and pull (static pressure), dust takes the easy path—into the hose. That means less cleanup, fewer filter clogs, and better visibility so you don’t miss low spots or create swirl marks.

Air behavior indoors amplifies the stakes. Warm motors create convection currents, HVAC returns draw particles into ductwork, and even your footsteps pump air like bellows across the floor. That’s why pre-planning is essential: turn off or seal returns, tape thresholds, and decide where extracted air will exhaust. You’re not just cleaning up after dust—you’re managing how air moves through the space while you work. With the right setup, your work zone becomes a one-way street for debris, from slab to bag, and your finish benefits from every minute the air stays clear.

Actionable tips:
- Close or tape off HVAC returns, under-door gaps, and wall penetrations before you start.
- Use work lights at low angles; clear air helps you see scratch patterns and missed trowel highs.
- Start farthest from your exit and work backward; you’ll stir up less settled dust.

## Picking vacuums, filters, and shrouds

Choosing a vacuum for indoor concrete work is about system balance. You need enough airflow to carry dust and chips through the hose, and enough static pressure (water lift) to overcome hose length, shroud restrictions, and filter loading. For a 5–7 inch grinder, plan for roughly 150–200 CFM at the tool with a 1.5–2 inch hose; for larger planetary machines, 250+ CFM with a 2-inch or dual-hose setup is common. But raw numbers aren’t the whole picture—dust type, runtime, and filter cleaning all affect real-world performance.

Filtration is non-negotiable. Look for true HEPA (tested capture down to 0.3 microns at 99.97%) and enough filter surface area that you’re not starving airflow as dust accumulates. Automatic filter cleaning—pulse or reverse air—keeps pores open, especially with fine concrete dust that cakes quickly. A pre-separator or cyclone upstream of the vacuum can drop 70–90% of heavier dust before it hits the HEPA stage, extending filter life and stabilizing CFM. Antistatic hoses not only reduce shocks; they help particles move smoothly, cutting clogs.

Shrouds are the unsung hero. A well-designed edge-trimmable shroud should float or skirt the floor with minimal gaps, flex over waviness, and keep a tight halo around the grinding path. A sloppy shroud leaks dust and air, forcing your vacuum to move room air instead of tool dust. Hose diameter and length matter too: longer runs and smaller diameters increase losses. If you must run long, step up a size and reduce at the tool. Keep bends gentle; sharp elbows are airflow killers.

Actionable tips:
- Match hose diameter to tool port; use a reducer or cuff to avoid “whistling” leaks.
- Prioritize vacuums with auto filter-clean; schedule manual blasts during bag changes.
- Use a pre-separator when dry grinding for more than 30 minutes at a stretch.

## Vacuum extraction for concrete polishing

Polishing is a process—progressing through grits while protecting the surface and your lungs. Vacuum extraction is what keeps each step honest. Start with a dry run-through: assemble the grinder, seat the shroud, check the skirt, and confirm the hose is firmly cuffed and strain-relieved so it won’t tug the shroud open. Power up the vacuum first, then the grinder; listen for the shroud to “kiss” the slab and watch for visible capture at the perimeter. If you see plume leakage, stop and tune before you commit to a full pass.

Set your workflow around the extractor. Plan two lanes: a main path for the grinder and a clean “return” for cords, hose, and you. Keep the hose off the floor with a strap or overhead hook; dragging a dusty hose across a polished section can scratch or dull your finish. As you shift grits, do a quick filter check—fine dust load rises with higher grits. Swap or shake pre-filters at logical breaks to avoid a sudden drop in CFM mid-pass. When edging, use a smaller grinder with a dedicated mini-shroud; alternately, remove the main shroud’s edge lip only where necessary and reseal immediately after.

Dust management continues when you stop. Use long-life collection bags that cinch closed before removal. Let the vacuum run for 10–15 seconds after shutting the grinder off to clear the hose. If your extractor has a pulse-clean cycle, trigger it before bagging out. Move filled bags directly into a second sealed contractor bag—less air, less mess.

## Beyond the tool: containment that works

Even perfect source capture benefits from good room strategy. Think of the space as a pressure map. Your extractor pulls air from the shroud; you can also encourage the room to feed that demand from clean directions. If possible, create a gentle negative pressure in the work zone by cracking a window or door on the far side of the space and using a secondary fan to draw makeup air in, while sealing the rest of the interior portal points with painter’s tape and plastic. This encourages any incidental leaks to track out of the building rather than into a hallway.

Build light containment where you must cross into living or occupied areas. A simple zipper-door barrier takes minutes to install and pays off immediately when you start moving tools in and out. Lay sticky mats at the exit and dedicate footwear to the work zone. Keep cords and hoses routed overhead or along one wall to reduce trip hazards and to prevent stirring settled dust with your feet. Place a shop broom and dry mop outside the zone—if dust escapes, catch it at the threshold.

Cleanup is part of containment. Resist the urge to blow off tools with compressed air; that just reheats the soup. Vac them instead with a brush attachment. For hard-to-reach cavities, a small crevice tool beats a leaf blower every time. If you do a wet mop at the end, make sure your sealer or densifier plan allows it; standing water before a densifier treatment can complicate chemistry. If you’re planning to densify between grit steps, keep it dry and let your extractor and a microfiber dust mop do the finish prep.

Practical steps:
- Tape HVAC returns and supply vents; open them only after the final clean.
- Use a 2-stage cleanup: vacuum everything, then microfiber mop in one direction.
- Keep a small HEPA vac on hand for touch-ups away from the grinder zone.

## Safety, compliance, and upkeep

Indoor grinding intersects with safety regulations for one reason: silica. Many regions align with a permissible exposure limit for respirable crystalline silica, and while a good extractor slashes airborne levels, personal protective equipment (PPE) still matters. Wear a fit-tested respirator with P100 or equivalent filters during grinding, plus eye and hearing protection. For multi-hour shifts or occupied buildings, consider real-time particle monitoring to validate air quality, especially near adjacent spaces.

Extractor maintenance impacts both performance and health. HEPA filters capture what you don’t want to breathe; handle them like hazardous material. Follow manufacturer schedules for bag changes and filter replacement. If the vacuum offers reverse-pulse cleaning, learn the cadence: too frequent and you waste time; too rare and airflow collapses. Keep spare filter sets sealed until use. Inspect hoses for pinholes—tiny leaks can drop static pressure below what your shroud needs and let dust leak at the skirt. Ground or use antistatic systems to reduce nuisance shocks and keep dust moving.

Electrical discipline matters indoors. Vacuums and grinders pull serious amperage; avoid daisy-chaining power strips and use appropriately gauged extension cords to limit voltage drop, which can sap motor torque and reduce airflow. Route cords where they won’t be abraded by the grinder or crushed under wheels. Finally, walk the space at lunch and at day’s end. A slow pass with the vac, a quick wipe of the grinder’s vents, and a look inside the shroud for skirt wear can save you from an afternoon of chasing preventable dust leaks.

Maintenance checklist:
- Pulse-clean or swap pre-filters before each new grit.
- Inspect shroud skirts for tears; replace segments as soon as you spot fraying.
- Log bag weights; sudden increases can signal a hose restriction or shroud gap.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q:** Can a regular shop vac handle indoor concrete grinding?  
**A:** Not reliably. Most shop vacs lack the airflow, static pressure, and filter surface to keep up with fine silica dust during sustained grinding. Look for a purpose-built extractor with HEPA filtration, auto filter cleaning, and a 1.5–2 inch antistatic hose.

**Q:** Do I need HEPA if I’m only polishing, not heavy grinding?  
**A:** Yes. Polishing still generates fine dust, especially at higher grits. True HEPA filtration captures the small, respirable particles that matter most for health and keeps indoor air and adjacent rooms cleaner.

**Q:** Wet grinding vs. dry with extraction—what’s better indoors?  
**A:** Dry with high-quality extraction usually wins for indoor work. Wet methods control airborne dust but create slurry that’s messy to manage, can affect densifier/sealer timing, and may introduce slip hazards. If you go wet, plan a thorough slurry containment and disposal process.

**Q:** How big should my vacuum be for a 7-inch grinder?  
**A:** Aim for 150–200 CFM at the tool, strong static pressure, auto filter-clean, and a tight shroud seal. Pair it with a 1.5–2 inch antistatic hose and, for longer runs, a pre-separator to maintain consistent airflow.

**Q:** What’s the fastest way to reduce dust tracking through the house?  
**A:** Build a simple containment zone: zipper door at the work entry, taped HVAC vents, sticky mats, and dedicated footwear. Keep the hose off finished floors and vacuum cords and tools before they cross the threshold.
